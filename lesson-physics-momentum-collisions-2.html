Momentum conservation and recoil

Internal forces always cancel in pairs, so an isolated system's total momentum never changes — which is why cannons recoil and rockets fly.

When it holds

Every internal force in a system has an equal and opposite partner (Newton's third law), so internal pushes shuffle momentum between parts of the system but never change the total. The total momentum of a system stays constant unless an outside force acts. Friction with the ground, gravity on a projectile, or a wall all count as outside.

m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2'

Center of mass

Total momentum equals total mass times the center-of-mass velocity. With no external force, the center of mass keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed no matter how violent the internal motion gets — an exploding shell's fragments scatter, but their center of mass traces the same arc the intact shell would have followed.

v_{\text{cm}} = \frac{m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2}

Worked example

A 1200 kg cannon fires a 15 kg cannonball horizontally at 400 m/s. Total momentum before firing is zero, so after firing it must still be zero: the cannon recoils at (15)(400)/1200 = 5.0 m/s backward. Both objects gained kinetic energy — burning powder paid for it — yet the momentum still sums to zero.

0 = (15)(400) + (1200)v \;\Rightarrow\; v = -5.0\ \text{m/s}

Flashcard

\sum \vec{p} = \text{constant}

Total momentum of an isolated system is fixed

Internal forces cancel in pairs, so only external forces move the total. This holds in every collision and explosion, elastic or not, which is why it is the go-to bookkeeping tool.

Check your understanding

A 40 kg child standing still on frictionless ice throws a 4.0 kg rock horizontally at 5.0 m/s. What is the child's recoil speed?
