Oscillator energy and pendulums

A harmonic oscillator's energy is fixed and just sloshes between potential and kinetic — and a small-swing pendulum keeps time regardless of mass.

Energy sloshing

Total energy E = (1/2)kA^2 is set by stiffness and amplitude, and it trades form continuously: all potential (1/2)kx^2 at the turning points x = plus or minus A, all kinetic (1/2)mv^2 at equilibrium. Damping leaks this budget away into heat and sound, which is why real oscillators need a periodic push to keep going.

E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^2

The pendulum

A pendulum's restoring force is gravity's tangential component, mg sin(theta), which is proportional to displacement only for small angles where sin(theta) is nearly theta in radians. That small-angle approximation gives T = 2 pi sqrt(L/g) — independent of both the bob's mass and the swing amplitude. Push a playground swing gently or hard and the period barely budges, until angles get large.

T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \quad (\text{small angles})

Worked example

Energy check: the 0.50 kg mass on the k = 200 N/m spring swinging with amplitude 0.10 m holds E = (1/2)(200)(0.10)^2 = 1.0 J. All 1.0 J is spring potential at the turning points, all 1.0 J is kinetic at the center, so v_max = sqrt(2E/m) = sqrt(2.0/0.50) = 2.0 m/s — matching A omega = (0.10)(20). Pendulum check: L = 1.0 m gives T = 2 pi sqrt(1.0/9.8) = 2.0 s, the classic seconds pendulum.

v_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.0)}{0.50}} = 2.0\ \text{m/s}

Flashcard

E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2

Total energy of a harmonic oscillator

Mechanical energy depends only on stiffness and amplitude. It all sits in the spring at the turning points and all in the motion at equilibrium, sloshing between the two twice per cycle.
