Magnetic forces and induction

Magnetism acts only on moving charge, and a changing magnetic flux is paid back with voltage — that trade runs every generator.

Magnetic force

A charge moving through a magnetic field feels a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the field: F = qvB sin(theta), biggest when the motion is perpendicular to B and zero when parallel. A current-carrying wire gets the same treatment, F = BIL sin(theta). Point your fingers along v or the current, curl them toward B, and your thumb gives the force on positive charge — the right-hand rule.

F = qvB\sin\theta

Faraday and Lenz

Faraday's law says a changing magnetic flux through a loop drives an EMF equal to the flux change per turn times the number of turns. Lenz's law is the minus sign: the induced current flows so its own field opposes the change in flux. That opposition is why generators demand mechanical work and why induction cannot deliver free energy.

\mathcal{E} = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}

Worked example

A 100-turn coil with area 0.010 m^2 sits in a 0.50 T field that drops to zero in 0.20 s. The flux per turn changes by (0.50)(0.010) = 5.0e-3 Wb, so the EMF is 100*(5.0e-3)/0.20 = 2.5 V while the field decays. By Lenz's law the induced current flows so its own field keeps pointing the way the external field used to — opposing the decrease.

\mathcal{E} = N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = \frac{(100)(5.0\times 10^{-3})}{0.20} = 2.5\ \text{V}

Flashcard

F = qvB\sin\theta

Force on a moving charge in a magnetic field

The force is perpendicular to both velocity and field, which makes magnetic forces bend paths into circles but do no work. It vanishes when the charge moves along the field lines, since sin(0) = 0.
