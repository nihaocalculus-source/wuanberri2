Elastic vs inelastic collisions

Momentum survives every collision; kinetic energy only survives the elastic ones — and in one dimension elastic collisions have a clean shortcut.

Perfectly inelastic

The extreme case: the objects stick together and share one final velocity. Momentum is still conserved even though kinetic energy is not — the missing energy went into denting, heating, and sound. Add the momenta before, divide by the combined mass after.

v' = \frac{m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2}

Elastic collisions

An elastic collision conserves kinetic energy as well as momentum. In one dimension this gives a shortcut: the relative velocity reverses, so the closing speed before equals the separating speed after. Equal masses are a special case — they simply swap velocities, which is how billiards pass speed down the line.

v_1 - v_2 = -(v_1' - v_2')

Worked example

Inelastic: a 2.0 kg block sliding at 6.0 m/s hits a 4.0 kg block at rest and they stick. Final speed is 12/6 = 2.0 m/s; kinetic energy drops from 36 J to 12 J, so 24 J went into the crunch. Elastic: a 2.0 kg ball at 3.0 m/s elastically hits a 1.0 kg ball at rest; they separate at 1.0 m/s and 4.0 m/s, which conserves both momentum (6 = 2 + 4, in kg m/s) and kinetic energy (9 J = 1 J + 8 J).

v_1' = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}v_1 = \frac{1}{3}(3.0) = 1.0\ \text{m/s}

Flashcard

v' = \frac{m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2}{m_1 + m_2}

Perfectly inelastic final velocity

When two objects fuse into one, divide the total incoming momentum by the total mass. This is the single most useful collision formula because so many real crashes approximate the stick-together case.
