Sequences and Infinite Sums

An infinite sum is really just a limit of partial sums, and that single idea explains when adding forever can still land on a finite number.

Sequence limits

A sequence is an infinite list of numbers written as a_n, and it converges to L if the terms get arbitrarily close to L as n grows large. For example, a_n = 1/n approaches 0, so the sequence converges to 0. If the terms grow without bound or oscillate, the sequence diverges, and any series built from its terms diverges too.

a_n = \frac{1}{n} \to 0

From list to sum

A series is what you get when you add the terms of a sequence, written as the sum of a_n from n equal 1 to infinity. The key object is the nth partial sum s_n, an ordinary finite sum of the first n terms. The series converges to S exactly when the sequence of partial sums converges to S.

s_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k

Partial sums example

Take the series 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + dots, where each term is half the one before it. The partial sums run 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, and so on, each step closing in on 1. Since s_n = 1 - (1/2)^n, the partial sums approach 1, so the infinite sum equals exactly 1. You can halve the remaining gap forever, and the gap shrinks to nothing.

Flashcard

a_n \to L

The sequence converges to the limit L

Convergence means that for any tolerance you pick, all terms far enough out in the list stay within that tolerance of L. The early terms do not matter; only the tail behavior decides the limit.
