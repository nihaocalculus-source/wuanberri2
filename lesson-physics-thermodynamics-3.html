Heat engines and entropy

Heat never flows uphill for free: every engine pays a tax to the cold reservoir, and entropy is the tax collector.

Heat engines

An engine takes heat Qh from a hot reservoir, converts some to work W, and dumps the rest Qc into a cold reservoir. Its efficiency is e = W/Qh = 1 - Qc/Qh. Carnot's theorem caps this at e = 1 - Tc/Th using absolute temperatures — no engine can beat a reversible one running between the same two reservoirs.

e_{max} = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h}

Refrigerators and entropy

A refrigerator runs the flow backwards: work input moves heat from the cold box into the warm room. Entropy change is delta-S = Q/T for heat Q entering at temperature T, and the second law says the universe's total entropy never decreases. That single inequality is why perfectly efficient engines and perpetual motion machines are impossible.

\Delta S_{universe} \ge 0

Worked example

An engine runs between a boiler at 500 K and the environment at 300 K. The Carnot bound is e = 1 - 300/500 = 0.40, so at best 40 percent of the absorbed heat becomes work. If the engine takes in 1000 J per cycle, the maximum work is 400 J and at least 600 J must be exhausted as waste heat.

e = 1 - \frac{300}{500} = 0.40

Flashcard

e = 1 - \frac{T_c}{T_h}

Carnot efficiency limit

Kelvin only: reservoirs at 227 C and 27 C mean 500 K and 300 K, giving 40 percent, not 88. Every real engine falls short of this bound because of friction and unrestrained heat flow.

An inventor claims an engine whose hot reservoir is at 227 C and whose cold reservoir is at 27 C. What is the maximum possible efficiency?
