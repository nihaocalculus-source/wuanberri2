Choosing a Convergence Test

A small toolkit of tests turns the vague question of whether a sum settles down into a routine you can run in a few lines.

Divergence test first

Always start here: if the terms a_n do not approach 0, the series must diverge. For example, the terms of n divided by (n+1) approach 1, so that series diverges immediately. The catch is that the test is one-way, since the harmonic series has terms approaching 0 and still diverges.

\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \neq 0 \implies \sum a_n \text{ diverges}

Ratio and comparison

The ratio test computes the limit of the absolute ratio of consecutive terms, call it L, and converges when L is below 1. For the series with terms n^2 divided by 2^n, the ratio limit is 1/2, so it converges. Comparison tests let you borrow a verdict from a simpler series with terms of similar size.

L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left| \frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} \right|

p-series benchmark

A p-series adds up 1 over n to the power p, and it converges exactly when p is greater than 1. The harmonic series is the p-series with p equal to 1, and the integral test shows its totals grow like the natural log, so it diverges. Memorizing the p-series cutoff gives you a reliable yardstick for comparison.

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^p} \text{ converges iff } p > 1

Flashcard

\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \neq 0

Term test forces divergence of the series

A sum of terms that do not shrink to zero cannot settle down, because every term keeps contributing a nonzero amount. Passing this test does not prove convergence, as the harmonic series shows.
