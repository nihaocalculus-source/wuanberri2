Momentum and collisions.

Momentum is mass in motion. It's conserved in every closed system — which is what makes collision problems solvable from a single equation, no matter how messy the geometry.

Linear momentum

A vector quantity: mass times velocity.

p = m · v

Units: kg · m/s. The direction of p matches the direction of v. A 1500 kg car moving at 20 m/s has p = 30,000 kg·m/s, eastward. Slow it down to 10 m/s and p drops to 15,000 — the impulse (change in momentum) is 15,000 kg·m/s.

Impulse

A force applied over a time changes momentum. The change is the impulse.

J = F · Δt = Δp

Why airbags save lives: a crash that would change momentum by 30,000 kg·m/s in 0.1 s needs 300,000 N of force. Spread it over 0.5 s and the force drops to 60,000 N — survivable. The momentum change is the same; only the time and the force change.

Conservation of momentum

In a closed system — no external forces — total momentum is constant. This is one of the deepest facts in physics, and it works whether the objects stick together, bounce apart, explode, or do anything in between.

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

The u's are initial velocities; the v's are final. One equation, two unknowns, and you can solve the whole collision.

Worked example

A 2 kg cart moving at 3 m/s collides with a stationary 1 kg cart and they stick together. What's their final velocity?

(2)(3) + (1)(0) = (2 + 1) vf 6 = 3 vf vf = 2 m/s

Total mass tripled; velocity dropped by a third. Momentum in = momentum out: 6 = 6. ✓
