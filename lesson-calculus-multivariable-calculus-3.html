The Gradient Points Uphill

The gradient packs all the partial derivatives into one vector that points in the direction of fastest increase.

The gradient vector

The gradient of f is the vector whose components are the partial derivatives, written nabla f. For the bowl-shaped function x squared plus y squared at the point (1, 1), the gradient is (2, 2), pointing away from the origin. Each point of a surface gets its own gradient, so it is a vector field rather than a single arrow.

\nabla f = \langle f_x,\, f_y \rangle

Directional derivative

The rate of change of f along a unit vector u is the dot product of the gradient with u, called the directional derivative. Continuing the example, along the diagonal direction (1 over root 2, 1 over root 2) at (1, 1), the dot product gives 2 root 2. Any direction with a smaller alignment with the gradient gives a smaller rate.

D_{\mathbf{u}} f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}

Slopes and level curves

The largest possible directional derivative at a point is the magnitude of the gradient, achieved by moving exactly along it. The gradient is also perpendicular to the level curve through that point, which is why contours and steepest paths cross at right angles. When the gradient is the zero vector, every direction has slope zero, marking a potential peak, valley, or saddle.

Vector collecting all first partial derivatives

The gradient turns a scalar function into a vector field. Its components are the slopes along the coordinate axes, and together they encode both the steepest direction and how steep it is.
